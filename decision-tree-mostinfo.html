What is a decision tree?

A decision tree is a flowchart of questions that ends in an answer. What makes it machine learning rather than just a flowchart is that nobody writes the questions. The algorithm reads your data and works out, all by itself, which questions to ask and in what order.

You already use decision trees every single day without calling them that.

When a doctor asks "do you have a fever?", then "how long has it lasted?", then "any rash?" — that is a decision tree. Each answer narrows the possibilities and points to the next question. Nobody asks about a rash first, because fever splits the crowd of possible illnesses much more usefully.

When you check whether a mango is ripe, you squeeze it, then smell the stem, then look at the colour. Same idea. A short chain of cheap questions, ending in a decision: buy it or leave it.

A decision tree algorithm does exactly this, except it discovers the best order of questions by measuring, on real data, which question separates things most cleanly.

The one-line definition

A supervised learning model that repeatedly splits data into smaller groups using yes/no questions about its features, until each group is pure enough to make a prediction.

It does two jobs

Classification — predicts a label. Spam or not spam. Approve or reject. Cat, dog, or horse.

Regression — predicts a number. House price. Tomorrow's temperature. Delivery time in minutes.

Twenty Questions, played well

Think of the party game. Your friend picks an animal and you get twenty yes/no questions to guess it. A beginner asks "Is it a golden retriever?" and burns a question to eliminate one possibility. An expert asks "Does it have fur?" and cuts the field roughly in half in a single move.

That instinct — ask the question that splits the group most evenly and most usefully — is the entire mathematics of decision trees. Everything technical that follows on this page is just a formal way of measuring which question is the "does it have fur?" of your dataset.

Where you'll actually meet them

Bank loan approvals. Insurance claim triage. Hospital readmission risk. Which customer is about to cancel their subscription. Which transaction looks fraudulent. Trees are popular in these places for one specific reason: when a regulator or a customer asks "why was I rejected?", you can print the exact path the model took. A neural network cannot do that.

Two minutes, out loud

Pick any everyday decision you make on autopilot — which route to take to work, whether to carry an umbrella, which chai stall to stop at. Write down the three questions your brain actually asks, in order.

Now ask yourself the important one: why is your first question first? Nine times out of ten, it is because that question rules out the most options. Congratulations, you just derived the root node selection rule.

Quiz — the big idea

0 / 3

Q1. What makes a decision tree a machine learning model rather than an ordinary flowchart?

An ordinary flowchart is authored by a person. A decision tree derives its questions, their order, and its cut-off points automatically by measuring which split separates the training data best.

Q2. In the Twenty Questions analogy, why is "does it have fur?" a better opening question than "is it a golden retriever?"

It is about how much the group shrinks. "Is it a golden retriever?" almost always returns No and eliminates one candidate. "Does it have fur?" eliminates roughly half the field whichever way it goes.

Q3. A tree that predicts tomorrow's electricity demand in megawatts is doing which job?

Regression. The output is a continuous number rather than a label, so the leaves store an average value instead of a class. We build one of these later on this page.
The vocabulary

The parts of a tree

Before we can talk about how a tree is built, we need names for its pieces. The whole vocabulary is borrowed from botany, with one strange twist: this tree grows downwards. The root sits at the top and the leaves hang at the bottom.

Hover or tap a label on the right to highlight that part of the diagram.

Root nodeThe very first question, asked of every single row in your data. It is chosen because it splits the data better than any other available question.
Internal nodeA follow-up question, asked only of the smaller slice of data that reached it. Also called a decision node or a split node.
Branch or edgeThe path you take out of a node depending on the answer. A binary tree has exactly two branches per node.
Leaf nodeA dead end, and a good one. No more questions get asked — the tree announces its prediction here.
DepthHow many questions deep the longest path runs. The root is depth 0. Depth is the single most important dial you will turn later.

Four more words worth knowing

Splitting

The act of dividing the rows at a node into two groups using one condition, such as credit_score == "Good" or age <= 34.5.

Parent and child

A node that splits is the parent. The two nodes it feeds are its children. Every node except the root has exactly one parent.

Subtree

Any node together with everything hanging beneath it. Pruning, later on, means chopping off a whole subtree and turning its top into a leaf.

Pure node

A node where every row shares the same answer — all Approved, or all Rejected. Purity is the goal of every split the tree makes.

An office org chart, upside down

The CEO at the top is the root and hears about everything. Managers below handle a slice each. The people at the bottom — the leaves — actually do the work and produce the output. Depth is the number of layers between the CEO and the ground floor. A very deep org chart is slow and fussy; a very flat one leaves the CEO deciding everything alone. That trade-off is exactly the one you will tune later.

Quiz — anatomy

0 / 3

Q1. How many parent nodes does the root node have?

Zero. The root is where everything begins, so nothing feeds into it. Every other node in the tree has exactly one parent.

Q2. A node contains 30 rows, and all 30 are labelled "Rejected". What do we call this node?

It is pure. There is no mixture left to separate, so asking another question would gain nothing. The tree stops here and predicts "Rejected".

Q3. A tree asks at most three questions before reaching any prediction. What is its depth?

Depth 3. Depth counts the number of splits along the longest root-to-leaf path. The root sits at level 0, so three questions means the deepest leaves live at level 3.
Using a finished tree

How a prediction actually happens

Building a tree is the hard part. Using one is almost insultingly simple: start at the root, answer its question, walk down the matching branch, repeat until you hit a leaf. Whatever the leaf says, that is your prediction.

Notice what this means in practice. Making a prediction costs a handful of comparisons — no matrix multiplication, no floating point heroics. A decision tree of depth 10 answers at most 10 questions no matter how many million rows it was trained on. This is why trees are used in latency-sensitive places like ad-serving and fraud checks.

It also means that a prediction always comes with a free explanation. The path is the reason. "Rejected because credit score was Bad, and income was below ₹40,000" is a sentence you can put in a letter to a customer.

Worth pausing on

Each row only ever visits one path. Rows do not go down both branches. This is why the groups at each level get smaller and smaller as you descend.

Q1. A trained tree has depth 6. How many questions will it ask about a brand new row, at most?

Six. A row travels down exactly one path. Depth 6 means at most six questions before it lands on a leaf, regardless of how wide the tree is.

The central problem

Which question should the root ask?

This is the whole algorithm in one sentence. A tree can only ask one question at the root, so it must decide: Income, or Credit score? Get this right and the rest of the tree is easy. Get it wrong and you waste a level.

TRY IT

Compare the two candidate splits

Splitting on Income gives you two groups of four, and each one is still 2 Yes and 2 No. You have done work and learned nothing. Both children are as mixed as the parent was. If you stopped here you would be guessing.

Splitting on Credit score gives you two groups of four as well, but one is entirely Yes and the other is entirely No. Both children are pure. One question and the problem is completely solved.

So the tree picks Credit score for the root. Now we need to make "the groups look cleaner" into a number a computer can compare, because on a real dataset with 200 features you cannot eyeball this.

Sorting laundry

You tip a basket of mixed washing onto the bed. You could sort by "does it belong to me or someone else?" and end up with two piles that are still a jumble of shirts, socks and towels. Or you could sort by colour, and end up with a whites pile and a darks pile — two groups where everything inside belongs together.

Both took one pass. Only one of them actually reduced the mess. A decision tree is looking for the colour sort, every single time.

The rule, stated plainly

At every node, the algorithm tries every feature and every possible cut-off within that feature, scores each candidate split by how much mess it removes, and keeps the winner. Then it does the same thing again on each child. That is it. That is the whole training algorithm, and the next three sections are just about how "mess" gets scored.

Quiz — choosing a split

0 / 3

Q1. Splitting our 8 applicants on Income gives two groups, each 2 Yes and 2 No. What has the tree gained?

Nothing useful. Smaller groups are not the goal — less mixed groups are. A split that leaves both children at the same 50/50 mixture has removed zero uncertainty.

Q2. When choosing a split, what does the algorithm actually search through?

An exhaustive search at each node. Every feature, every candidate threshold, scored and compared. Random feature selection does exist, but it is a Random Forest trick, not standard tree behaviour.

Q3. Two groups are described below. Which one is "purer"?

Group B. Purity is about how lopsided the label mixture is, not how many rows there are. A 9-to-1 group is nearly settled; a 5-to-5 group is a coin flip.
The key concept

Impurity: putting a number on "mixed up"

Impurity measures how jumbled the labels inside a group are. Zero means perfectly pure — everything in the group shares one label. The higher the number, the more of a mess you are looking at.

The intuition first

Picture a jar of marbles. A jar with 20 green marbles is pure — reach in blindfolded and you know exactly what you will pull out. A jar with 10 green and 10 red is maximally impure — you have no idea. A jar with 19 green and 1 red is nearly pure; you would bet on green and usually win.

Impurity is just a formula that turns that feeling of "how confidently could I guess?" into a number between 0 and something.

The two measures you will meet

Gini impurity — the probability of labelling a randomly drawn item wrongly, if you guessed using the group's own proportions. Fast, and scikit-learn's default.

Entropy — borrowed from information theory. It measures, in bits, how much genuine surprise is left in the group. Slightly slower to compute because of the logarithm.

Notice the ceilings

Quiz — impurity

0 / 3

Q1. A node has an impurity of exactly 0. What does that tell you?

The node is pure. One label, no mixture, no uncertainty. Splitting it further cannot improve anything, so the tree turns it into a leaf.

Q2. For a two-class problem, what is the largest possible Gini impurity?

0.5, reached at a 50/50 split. Entropy reaches 1.0 at that same point, which is exactly why the two scales must never be compared to each other directly.

Q3. Which group has the higher impurity?

18 / 14 is the messiest. It sits closest to an even split, so it is hardest to guess. Group size does not enter into the impurity calculation at all — only the proportions do.
The maths, part one

Gini impurity, worked out by hand

Gini answers one question: if I picked a random row from this group and guessed its label using the group's own proportions, how often would I be wrong?

Gini = 1 − Σ (pi)²
where pi is the fraction of the group belonging to class i

Square each class fraction, add them up, subtract from one. That is the entire calculation. Let us run it on our loan data.

Step 1 — impurity before any split

All 8 applicants: 4 Yes 4 No

p(Yes) = 4/8 = 0.5
p(No)  = 4/8 = 0.5
Gini = 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²)
Gini = 1 − (0.25 + 0.25)
Gini = 0.500

0.500 is the worst possible score for a two-class problem. Our starting point is a perfect coin flip, which is honest — with no questions asked, we genuinely have no idea whether a new applicant gets approved.

This number is our baseline. Every candidate split will now be judged on how far below 0.500 it can drag the impurity.

Step 2 — score the split on Income

Income = High → rows 1, 2, 3, 7

2 Yes 2 No

Gini = 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.500

Income = Low → rows 4, 5, 6, 8

2 Yes 2 No

Gini = 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.500

Now combine the children into one score. Bigger groups matter more, so we take a weighted average using each child's share of the rows:

Ginisplit = (4/8 × 0.500) + (4/8 × 0.500) = 0.500
Improvement over the baseline: 0.500 − 0.500 = 0.000. Absolutely nothing gained.

Step 3 — score the split on Credit score

Credit = Good → rows 1, 2, 4, 8

4 Yes 0 No

Gini = 1 − (1.0² + 0²) = 0.000

Credit = Bad → rows 3, 5, 6, 7

0 Yes 4 No

Gini = 1 − (0² + 1.0²) = 0.000
Ginisplit = (4/8 × 0.000) + (4/8 × 0.000) = 0.000
Improvement over the baseline: 0.500 − 0.000 = 0.500. The mess is completely gone.
The verdict

Credit score wins by a mile, and it wins by an amount we can now defend with a number rather than a feeling. This drop in impurity has a name — Gini gain, or more generally impurity decrease. The tree always picks the split with the largest one.

Quiz — Gini

0 / 3

Q1. A node holds 6 Yes and 2 No. What is its Gini impurity?

0.375. p(Yes) = 0.75 and p(No) = 0.25, so Gini = 1 − (0.5625 + 0.0625) = 0.375.

Q2. Why is the child impurity a weighted average rather than a plain one?

Group size matters. A split producing a pure child of 1 row and a messy child of 99 has barely helped. Weighting by row count keeps the score honest about that.

Q3. Baseline Gini is 0.48. Split A gives a weighted Gini of 0.30, split B gives 0.12. Which does the tree choose?

Split B. Its gain is 0.48 − 0.12 = 0.36 against split A's 0.18. The tree is always hunting for the biggest impurity decrease it can find.
The maths, part two

Entropy and information gain

Entropy measures the same mess as Gini but in a different currency: bits of surprise. It comes from information theory, and it asks how many yes/no questions you would need, on average, to pin down the label of a random row.

Entropy = − Σ pi · log2(pi)
and Information Gain = Entropy(parent) − weighted Entropy(children)

The same three steps

Before splitting

4 Yes, 4 No → p = 0.5, 0.5
E = −(0.5 · log₂0.5) − (0.5 · log₂0.5)
E = −(0.5 × −1) − (0.5 × −1)
E = 1.000 bit

After splitting on Credit score

Good branch: 4 Yes, 0 No → E = 0.000
Bad branch:  0 Yes, 4 No → E = 0.000
Weighted = (0.5 × 0) + (0.5 × 0) = 0.000
Information Gain = 1.000 − 0.000 = 1.000 bit

After splitting on Income

Both branches: 2 Yes, 2 No → E = 1.000 each
Weighted = 1.000
Information Gain = 1.000 − 1.000 = 0.000 bits

Guessing a coin versus guessing a loaded die

A fair coin carries exactly 1 bit of uncertainty — one yes/no question ("heads?") settles it. A coin that lands heads 99% of the time carries almost no uncertainty; you would rarely need to ask.

Entropy measures that. One bit of information gain means the split has removed a whole coin flip's worth of doubt from the problem. That is why entropy is sometimes described as the amount of surprise left in a group.

Handy landmark values

50/50 → 1.000 bit. 75/25 → 0.811. 90/10 → 0.469. 100/0 → 0.000. Notice how slowly it falls at first: going from a 50/50 to a 75/25 mixture only removes about a fifth of the uncertainty.

Where the name ID3 comes from

The original decision tree algorithms — ID3 and its successor C4.5, built by Ross Quinlan in the 1980s — used information gain exactly as written above. Modern scikit-learn uses CART, which defaults to Gini but will happily use entropy if you pass criterion="entropy".

Compute one by hand

A node contains 3 Yes and 1 No. Work out its entropy. You will need log₂(0.75) ≈ −0.415 and log₂(0.25) = −2.

E = −(0.75 × −0.415) − (0.25 × −2)
E = 0.311 + 0.500
E = 0.811 bits

For comparison, the Gini of that same node is 1 − (0.5625 + 0.0625) = 0.375. Different numbers, same message: this group leans strongly towards Yes but is not settled yet.

Entropy and information gain

Quiz — entropy

0 / 3

Q1. What is the entropy of a node containing 10 Yes and 0 No?

Zero. There is no surprise left. You already know the answer before you look, so no questions are needed to determine the label.

Q2. Information gain is defined as which of these?

It is a before-minus-after measurement. How much uncertainty existed before the question, less how much survives it. Bigger gain means a more useful question.

Q3. A split produces an information gain of 0.00. What does that mean?

A wasted question. This is exactly what happened with Income on our loan data: two smaller groups, the same 50/50 mixture in each, and no progress made.
A question everyone asks

Gini or entropy — does the choice matter?

The short answer

Almost never. Studies comparing the two on real datasets find they disagree on the chosen split in roughly 2% of cases, and when they do disagree the resulting accuracy difference is usually negligible.

Both are zero at purity, both peak at an even mixture, and both fall as a group becomes lopsided. They are two different curves telling the same story.

The practical answer

Use Gini. It is the default, it avoids computing logarithms, and it is therefore a little faster on large datasets. Reach for entropy when you are teaching information theory, replicating an ID3 or C4.5 result, or a paper you are reproducing specifies it.

When to reach for which

Use Gini when — you just want a working classifier, you are on a large dataset where training speed matters, or you have no specific reason to prefer anything else. This covers the large majority of real projects.

Use entropy when — you are explicitly working with information gain (ID3/C4.5 style trees), teaching or learning the information-theoretic view, comparing against a paper or textbook that specifies it, or you want slightly more sensitivity to splits on already-lopsided nodes.

Quiz — comparing the two

0 / 2

Q1. A colleague reports "my node has impurity 0.7, so entropy must be worse than Gini here". What is wrong with that?

Different units entirely. Two-class Gini maxes out at 0.5 and entropy at 1.0. A reading of 0.7 is impossible for Gini and merely middling for entropy. Always compare like with like.

Q2. Which is scikit-learn's default criterion for DecisionTreeClassifier?

Gini, because it is cheaper to compute. entropy and log_loss are both available for classification; squared_error is the default over on the regressor side.
A detail that trips people up

What happens with numbers instead of categories?

Our loan table used tidy categories — High or Low, Good or Bad. Real data is full of numbers: age 34, income ₹58,200, 17 previous transactions. A number has infinitely many possible cut-off points, so how does the tree pick one?

The answer is neater than you might expect. The tree does not consider infinitely many thresholds. It only needs to consider the points where the label could possibly change.

  1. Sort the rows by that feature's value.
  2. Take the midpoint between each pair of neighbouring values. Eight distinct values give at most seven candidate thresholds.
  3. Score every candidate with Gini or entropy, exactly as before.
  4. Keep the winner, and store it as a condition like income <= 31.5.

Any threshold between two neighbouring values produces the identical split, so testing the midpoint tests them all. That is what makes an infinite search finite.

Three consequences worth remembering

No scaling needed. A tree only ever asks "is this value above or below that one?" Multiplying every income by 1,000 changes the threshold and nothing else. This is why trees skip the StandardScaler step that most other models demand.

Outliers barely matter. One applicant earning ₹90 lakh is just "the rightmost dot". It cannot drag a mean around, because no mean is ever computed.

Monotone transforms are free. Take the log of a feature and the tree finds an equivalent split, because the ordering of the rows has not changed.

Setting the pass mark

You are told to draw a single line through a sorted list of exam scores to separate students who passed the practical from those who failed. You would not test every real number between 0 and 100. You would slide the line to sit between adjacent scores and check which position separates them best.

A decision tree does exactly this, for every numeric feature, at every node.

Quiz — numeric splits

0 / 3

Q1. A numeric feature has 12 distinct values in a node. How many candidate thresholds does the tree evaluate?

Eleven midpoints. Every threshold between the same two neighbouring values yields an identical split, so testing one representative point per gap covers the entire real line.

Q2. Do you need to standardise or normalise features before fitting a decision tree?

No scaling required. Distance-based models like KNN and SVM care deeply about scale. Trees ask only "above or below this threshold?", which is unaffected by units.

Q3. One row in your data has an income of ₹5 crore while every other row is under ₹15 lakh. What happens to the tree?

Trees are naturally robust to outliers on the feature side. Ordering is all that matters, and one extreme value changes the ordering of nothing else. Linear regression would not be so forgiving.
Putting it together

How the whole tree gets built

We now have every piece. Growing a full tree is just the split-scoring routine from the last few sections, applied over and over on smaller and smaller groups until there is nothing left worth splitting.

STEP THROUGH

The algorithm, one line at a time

This is called recursive binary splitting

Recursive, because the procedure calls itself on each child it creates. Binary, because scikit-learn always makes exactly two branches per node.

It is also greedy: at each node it takes the best split available right now, never checking whether a slightly worse split today would open up a much better one two levels down. Finding the truly optimal tree is computationally infeasible, so greedy is what everybody uses.

Greedy, as in driving without a map

At every junction you take the road that looks like it heads towards your destination. Usually fine. Occasionally you would have arrived far sooner by turning briefly away from your goal to reach a highway. A decision tree never takes that detour, and this is one root cause of the instability we discuss shortly.

When does it stop?

Natural stops

The node is pure, or every row in it has identical feature values so no split is even possible.

Rules you impose

Maximum depth reached, too few rows to split, or a leaf would end up smaller than the minimum you allowed.

Nothing left to gain

The best available split reduces impurity by less than your min_impurity_decrease threshold, so it is not worth taking.

The dangerous default

If you set none of those limits, scikit-learn keeps splitting until every single leaf is pure. On a real dataset that can mean hundreds of leaves holding one row each. Training accuracy hits 100%, which feels wonderful for about ten seconds, until you score it on data it has never seen. That is the subject of the next section.

Quiz — the algorithm

0 / 3

Q1. What does "greedy" mean in the context of tree building?

Locally optimal, globally uncertain. Searching every possible tree is NP-hard, so all practical implementations settle for the best split at each step and accept that the finished tree may not be the best one possible.

Q2. With no depth or size limits set, when does scikit-learn stop growing a classification tree?

It grows all the way down. max_depth defaults to None, which is precisely why an unconstrained tree is such a reliable overfitter.

Q3. The procedure is described as "recursive" because —

Same procedure, smaller data. Find best split, create two children, then run find-best-split on each child. The recursion unwinds when a stopping condition fires.
The other half of the family

Trees that predict numbers

Everything so far predicted a category. Swap two components and the same machine predicts a continuous number instead — house prices, delivery times, tomorrow's demand.

What changes

The impurity measure. Gini and entropy are meaningless for numbers, so the tree measures variance instead — usually mean squared error. A group of house prices all clustered near ₹80 lakh has low variance; a group scattered from ₹20 lakh to ₹4 crore has high variance.

What the leaf stores. A classification leaf stores the majority class. A regression leaf stores the average of the target values that landed there.

What stays exactly the same

The greedy search. The exhaustive scan over features and thresholds. The recursion. The stopping rules. The overfitting problem, and the pruning that fixes it.

In scikit-learn you literally change one class name: DecisionTreeClassifier becomes DecisionTreeRegressor, and criterion defaults to "squared_error".

The shape of a regression tree's predictions

A regression tree predicts a flat value per leaf, so its output looks like a staircase, not a smooth line. Feed it a perfectly straight relationship such as y = 2x and it will approximate it with steps. Add more depth and the steps get finer, but they never truly become a line. This is a genuine weakness — and the reason linear models still win on genuinely linear problems.

Q1. What does a leaf in a regression tree store?

The average. Every new row landing on that leaf receives that same number as its prediction, which is why the output looks like a staircase.

Q2. Which criterion replaces Gini when predicting a continuous value?

Variance reduction. The tree hunts for the split that leaves each child's values most tightly clustered around their own mean. squared_error is scikit-learn's default here.

Q3. Why does a regression tree struggle with a perfectly straight relationship like y = 2x?

Constant leaves, staircase output. Deeper trees give finer steps but never a true slope. On genuinely linear problems, linear regression will beat a tree with far less effort.
The famous weakness

Overfitting: when a tree memorises instead of learning

Left unsupervised, a decision tree will keep splitting until it has built a personalised rule for every single training row. It scores 100% on the data it studied and falls apart on anything new.

Why trees are unusually prone to this

Most models have limited flexibility baked in. A linear regression can only ever draw a straight line, so there is a ceiling on how badly it can contort itself around noise.

A tree has no such ceiling. Give it enough depth and it can carve the feature space into as many boxes as there are rows. It has the capacity to memorise your dataset exactly, and if you do not stop it, it will.

The student who memorised the question papers

Two students prepare for an exam. One learns the concepts. The other memorises the last ten years of question papers, answer by answer.

On a mock test drawn from those papers, the memoriser scores higher. Then the real exam arrives with reworded questions, and they collapse — while the first student, who scored lower on the mock, handles it fine. Training accuracy is the mock test. Test accuracy is the real exam.

How to spot it in your own work

A large gap between training and test scores is the tell. 99% training and 71% test is not a good model with a small flaw — it is a memoriser. As a rough guide, a gap beyond about 5 to 10 percentage points deserves investigation. Also check the tree itself: leaves containing one or two rows each are a strong symptom.

The trade-off underneath

Bias and variance, in dart-throwing terms

Underfitting and overfitting are two ends of one spectrum, and the classic way to picture it is a dartboard. Each dart is a model trained on a slightly different sample of data.

High bias — a shallow tree

All the darts land tightly together, but well away from the bullseye. Retrain on new data and you get almost the same wrong answer every time.

The model is consistent and consistently too simple. This is underfitting, and a tree of depth 1 — a "decision stump" — is the extreme case.

High variance — a deep tree

The darts scatter all over the board. On average they surround the bullseye, but no individual throw can be trusted.

Change a handful of training rows and you get a visibly different tree with different splits. This is overfitting, and it is a fully grown tree's natural state.

Why this matters specifically for trees

Trees sit unusually far towards the high-variance end. Because the root split is chosen greedily, swapping a few rows can change the root — and once the root changes, every node beneath it changes too. One different question at the top can rewrite the whole tree. Remember this: it is the entire motivation for Random Forests.

Two weather forecasters

One always says "22 degrees and partly cloudy" no matter the season. Reliable, and reliably wrong in December. That is bias.

The other rewrites the forecast dramatically after every gust of wind. Sometimes brilliantly accurate, sometimes wildly off, and you can never tell which day you are getting. That is variance. A good model, like a good forecaster, sits between the two.

The fix

Pruning: stopping a tree from growing wild

Pruning means deliberately keeping the tree smaller than it wants to be. There are two moments at which you can intervene: while it grows, or after it has finished.

Pre-pruning, also called early stopping

Set rules up front and the tree simply refuses to grow past them. This is what you will use 90% of the time, because it is cheap and easy to reason about.

  • max_depth — the hard ceiling on levels
  • min_samples_split — refuse to split a node holding fewer than this many rows
  • min_samples_leaf — refuse any split that would create a leaf smaller than this
  • max_leaf_nodes — cap the total number of leaves
  • min_impurity_decrease — reject splits that barely help

The catch: it is greedy about stopping too. It may cut off a branch that looked unpromising but would have led somewhere valuable one level further down.

Post-pruning, also called cost-complexity pruning

Grow the tree fully, then work back up and remove subtrees that do not earn their keep. Each candidate removal is scored by how much accuracy it costs against how much simplicity it buys.

In scikit-learn this is ccp_alpha. At 0.0 nothing is pruned; as you raise it, more and more subtrees collapse into leaves.

The advantage: it sees the whole tree before deciding, so it does not mistakenly cut a branch that turned out to matter. The cost is that you grow a big tree first.

When to reach for which

Use pre-pruning when — the dataset is large and growing a full tree first would be slow, you already have a rough sense of a sensible depth or leaf size, you need something explainable and want a hard, predictable limit on size, or you are doing quick iteration and want a cheap first guard against overfitting.

Use post-pruning when — you want the best accuracy you can get and can afford to grow a full tree first, you are not sure in advance how much complexity the data actually needs, or you have already tried pre-pruning and suspect it is cutting off branches too early. In practice, many people do both: loose pre-pruning limits to keep training fast, then ccp_alpha for the final trim.

Pruning an actual tree in your garden

A gardener does not cut branches at random. They remove the growth that is crowded, weak, or drawing energy without producing fruit. The tree ends up smaller and healthier, and next season it fruits better than the overgrown version would have.

Same logic here. Those tiny leaves holding two rows each are branches drawing energy and producing noise. Cut them and the model generalises better, even though its training accuracy goes down.

Finding a good ccp_alpha in practice

Call clf.cost_complexity_pruning_path(X_train, y_train) to get the list of alpha values at which the tree structure actually changes. Fit one tree per alpha, plot training and test accuracy against alpha, and pick the value just before test accuracy starts to fall. It is a short loop and it usually beats guessing at max_depth.

Choose the intervention

For each situation, decide which parameter you would reach for first.

1. Your tree has 400 leaves and many hold a single row.
2. The tree is 22 levels deep and impossible to explain to a stakeholder.
3. Accuracy is fine but the tree keeps splitting on splits that reduce impurity by 0.0002.

1 → min_samples_leaf, set to something like 5 or 10. It directly forbids the single-row leaves that are the symptom here.

2 → max_depth, set to 4 or 5. If the tree must be explainable to a human, depth is the constraint that matters most, sometimes more than accuracy.

3 → min_impurity_decrease, set to a small positive value such as 0.001. It filters out splits that are technically improvements but statistically meaningless.

Q1. What is the essential difference between pre-pruning and post-pruning?

Timing is the difference. Pre-pruning is cheaper because you never build the branches. Post-pruning is better informed because it can see the finished tree before deciding what to cut.

Q2. What happens as you increase ccp_alpha?

Higher alpha means more aggressive pruning. At 0.0 the tree stays fully grown. As alpha rises, subtrees whose accuracy contribution does not justify their complexity are collapsed into leaves.

Q3. After pruning, training accuracy drops from 100% to 92% while test accuracy rises from 70% to 87%. Was pruning worth it?

Clearly worth it. Training accuracy measures memory; test accuracy measures usefulness. Trading 8 points of the first for 17 points of the second is exactly the trade pruning exists to make.
From theory to code

Building one in scikit-learn

Everything on this page so far happens inside about six lines of Python. Here is the whole workflow, with the pieces you have just learned marked in the comments.

from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, plot_tree, export_text
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

X, y = load_iris(return_X_y=True)

# hold back 30% that the tree never sees during training
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=42, stratify=y
)

clf = DecisionTreeClassifier(
    criterion="gini",      # or "entropy" — the impurity measure
    max_depth=4,            # pre-pruning: hard ceiling on levels
    min_samples_split=10,   # pre-pruning: need 10 rows to even try a split
    min_samples_leaf=5,      # pre-pruning: no leaf smaller than 5 rows
    random_state=42         # reproducible tie-breaking
)
clf.fit(X_train, y_train)

# always score both — the gap between them is the story
print("train:", accuracy_score(y_train, clf.predict(X_train)))
print("test :", accuracy_score(y_test,  clf.predict(X_test)))

# read the tree as plain text, or draw it
print(export_text(clf, feature_names=list(load_iris().feature_names)))
plot_tree(clf, filled=True, feature_names=load_iris().feature_names)
plt.show()

fit

Runs the recursive splitting we walked through. Everything — impurity scoring, threshold search, stopping rules — happens inside this one call.

predict

Walks each row from root to leaf and returns the leaf's stored answer. Use predict_proba to get the class proportions inside the leaf instead.

export_text

Prints the entire tree as indented if/else rules. The fastest way to sanity-check what your model actually learned.

One habit worth forming

Print both training and test accuracy every single time, not just test. A single test number tells you how good the model is; the gap tells you why, and what to change next. Most tuning decisions in this section come straight from reading that gap.

Predict the output before you run it

Suppose you delete max_depth, min_samples_split and min_samples_leaf from the code above, leaving only criterion and random_state. What will the two printed accuracies look like?

Training accuracy will be 1.0. With no limits, the tree splits until every leaf is pure, so it classifies every training row it has already seen perfectly.

Test accuracy will drop. On Iris the fall is modest, since the dataset is small and clean — but on messier real data this is where a 30-point gap appears. Try it both ways and watch the numbers move.

The control panel

Every knob, and what it does

You will meet these in every tree-based model you ever tune, including Random Forests and gradient boosting. Tap any one to open it.

Which impurity measure scores each split. gini (default), entropy, or log_loss for classification; squared_error, friedman_mse, absolute_error or poisson for regression. Rarely the parameter that decides your result.
The hard ceiling on levels. Defaults to None, meaning unlimited, which is the single most common cause of an overfitted tree. Start around 3 to 6 and tune from there. If a human needs to read the tree, keep it at 4 or below.
The minimum rows a node must hold before the tree is even allowed to consider splitting it. Default 2, which is extremely permissive. Values of 10 to 20 are a reasonable starting point on real datasets. Accepts a float to mean a fraction of all rows.
The minimum rows any resulting leaf must contain. Default 1, which permits a leaf built from a single row — pure memorisation. Raising this to 5 or more smooths the model and is often more effective than capping depth.
How many features to consider at each split. Default is all of them. Restricting it speeds up training on wide datasets and, crucially, is the mechanism that makes the trees inside a Random Forest different from one another.
A direct cap on the total number of leaves. When set, the tree grows best-first rather than depth-first, expanding whichever node offers the largest impurity reduction. Useful when you care about total model size rather than depth.
A split only goes ahead if it reduces weighted impurity by at least this much. Default 0.0, so any improvement counts however trivial. Setting 0.001 to 0.01 filters out cosmetic splits that only fit noise.
Post-pruning strength. Default 0.0 prunes nothing. Raise it and subtrees that do not pay for their complexity get collapsed into leaves. Pair it with cost_complexity_pruning_path to find sensible candidate values rather than guessing.
Tells the tree to care more about an under-represented class. Set to "balanced" when, say, only 2% of your rows are fraud — otherwise the tree happily predicts "not fraud" for everything and reports 98% accuracy.
Fixes the randomness used when several splits score identically. It does not make the model better, only repeatable. Always set it in anything you intend to share or compare.
A sane tuning order

Start with max_depth, since it moves the needle most. Then min_samples_leaf. Then, if the class balance is skewed, class_weight. Everything else is fine-tuning. Wrap the lot in GridSearchCV with cross-validation rather than adjusting one value at a time against a single test split — otherwise you slowly overfit to the test set yourself.

class_weight. It reweights the impurity calculation so that misclassifying a rare fraud case costs the tree far more than misclassifying a common legitimate one.

min_samples_leaf. It forbids any split that would leave a leaf smaller than the value you set, which eliminates the single-row leaves at source.

Impurity-based importance is biased towards features with many distinct values. Where a single tree is the right call

Explainability is the point

Credit decisions, medical triage, anything a regulator or a customer will ask you to justify in writing.

Mixed, messy features

Numbers and categories side by side, wildly different scales, outliers you have not cleaned yet.

A fast first baseline

Fit one in seconds, read the importances, and learn what your data actually contains before building anything sophisticated.

When to reach for something else instead

The relationship is smooth or linear

A tree can only output flat, boxy steps. If y really does move proportionally with x, linear or logistic regression will fit it better with far less machinery.

You need stable, low-variance predictions

A single tree can change shape noticeably after a handful of rows are added or removed. Where consistency matters more than a whiteboard-friendly explanation, a Random Forest or gradient-boosted ensemble is the safer default.

The data is very high-dimensional and sparse

Text represented as thousands of rare word-count columns, for instance. Axis-parallel splits struggle to find signal one column at a time; linear models or naive Bayes usually do better.

Raw accuracy is all that matters

If nobody ever needs to see the logic and you just want the best possible score, an ensemble or a well-tuned gradient boosting library will almost always beat one tree.

You need to extrapolate

Predicting outside the range of values the tree was trained on — future years, prices beyond anything seen before — a tree just repeats its nearest leaf's answer. A model with an explicit trend term won't.

Your dataset is tiny

With very few rows a tree has almost nothing to generalise from and will latch onto noise. A simpler, more constrained model tends to be more reliable until you have more data.

Q1. A customer ID column shows the highest feature importance in your tree. What is going on?

A well-known trap. With one distinct value per row, an ID can carve the data into pure leaves and score beautifully on training data while carrying zero predictive signal. Drop identifier columns before fitting.

Q2. Your relationship between x and y is a clean straight line. Which model is the better choice?

Q3. You need to predict sales for a year further ahead than any year in your training data. Why is a decision tree a poor choice here?

No extrapolation. Every leaf stores a fixed number learned from training rows. Outside that range, the tree just returns its closest leaf's answer instead of continuing a trend — a model with an explicit slope handles this far better.
Where this leads next

One tree is unstable. Many trees are not.

We saw that changing a handful of rows can rewrite an entire tree. That single weakness launched the most successful family of algorithms in applied machine learning.

Bagging

Train many trees, each on a random sample of the rows drawn with replacement, then average their votes. Individual trees stay unstable; the average is far steadier.

Random Forest

Bagging, plus each split is only allowed to consider a random subset of features. This stops every tree from picking the same dominant feature at the root and forces genuine diversity.

Gradient Boosting

Trees built one after another, each correcting the errors of those before it. XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost all work this way, and they still win most competitions on tabular data.

One expert, or a room full of them

A single specialist gives you a confident answer that might be badly wrong. Ask fifty reasonably competent people independently and average their answers, and the individual errors tend to cancel while the shared signal survives. Random Forests are exactly this, with decision trees as the panel.

Why we studied one tree first

Every one of those methods is built from the exact machinery on this page: impurity, greedy splitting, depth, pruning. Understanding a single tree properly means Random Forests and boosting become variations on something familiar rather than new algorithms to memorise.

Q1. What problem with single trees does a Random Forest primarily solve?

Variance reduction through averaging. Each tree is still unstable, but their errors are partly independent, so combining them cancels much of the noise. The cost is losing the readable single decision path.

Q2. How does gradient boosting differ from bagging?

Sequential correction versus parallel averaging. Bagging trains independent trees and averages them; boosting trains each new tree specifically on what the current ensemble is still getting wrong.

Q1. A node holds 8 Yes and 2 No. What is its Gini impurity?

0.32. 1 − (0.8² + 0.2²) = 1 − (0.64 + 0.04) = 0.32.

Q2. Splitting a node produces two children with weighted impurity equal to the parent's. What should the tree do?

Zero gain, no split. Exactly what happened with Income on our loan data: two smaller groups, the same mixture, no progress.

Q3. Which of these does not reduce overfitting?

random_state only affects reproducibility. It shifts which tree you get when splits tie, but does nothing to limit the tree's capacity to memorise.

Q4. A numeric feature holds the values 10, 20, 20, 35 and 60 in a node. How many candidate thresholds are evaluated?

Three. There are four distinct values — 10, 20, 35, 60 — giving three gaps and therefore three midpoints: 15, 27.5 and 47.5. The duplicate 20 adds no new cut point.

Q5. Which statement about regression trees is true?

Variance in, mean out. Gini and entropy have no meaning for continuous targets, so squared error takes their place, and each leaf predicts the average of the rows that reached it.

Q6. Model A: 100% train, 62% test. Model B: 88% train, 85% test. Which would you deploy?

Model B, comfortably. Model A has memorised its training set. Test performance is the only figure that predicts behaviour on tomorrow's data, and B wins it by 23 points with a healthy 3-point gap.
